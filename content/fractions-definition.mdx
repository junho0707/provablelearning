---
summary: What a fraction really means — equal parts of a whole — and what the numerator and denominator each tell you.
---

A **fraction** is a way of naming *part of a whole*. When we split something into equal pieces
and take some of them, a fraction records exactly how many we took and how many make the whole.

## The two numbers

Every fraction is written as one number over another:

$$
\frac{\text{numerator}}{\text{denominator}}
$$

- The **denominator** (the bottom) tells you how many **equal parts** the whole is split into.
- The **numerator** (the top) tells you how many of those parts you have.

So in $\frac{3}{4}$, the whole is cut into $4$ equal parts and we are counting $3$ of them. Read it
aloud as "three quarters."

## Why the parts must be equal

The idea only works if the pieces are the *same size*. If you cut a pizza into four uneven slices,
taking one is not really $\frac{1}{4}$ — one slice might be far more than a quarter of the pizza.
Equal parts are what make a fraction a fair, exact measurement.

## The whole as a fraction

If you take **all** the parts, you have the whole thing back again:

$$
\frac{4}{4} = 1
$$

Whenever the numerator equals the denominator, the fraction equals $1$. And $\frac{0}{4} = 0$ —
taking none of the parts leaves you with nothing.

That is the whole idea of a fraction: **equal parts of a whole**, with the bottom number setting
the size of each part and the top number counting how many you have.
